Hex

Simple rules, infinite depth

History

Hex was invented independently by two brilliant minds:

  • Piet Hein (1942) — Danish mathematician, poet, and inventor. He called it "Polygon" and introduced it at the Niels Bohr Institute.
  • John Nash (1948) — American mathematician, Nobel laureate, subject of A Beautiful Mind. He reinvented it at Princeton, where it became a campus obsession played on the hexagonal bathroom tiles.

Martin Gardner featured it in his Scientific American "Mathematical Games" column in 1957, cementing its place in puzzle history. Parker Brothers marketed it as "Hex" in 1952.

Rules

  • Board: An N×N rhombus of hexagonal cells. Standard sizes are 11×11 (quick) and 13×13 (tournament).
  • Players: Black connects top ↔ bottom. White connects left ↔ right.
  • Black moves first. Then alternating turns.
  • On your turn, place one stone on any empty hexagon. Once placed, stones never move or get captured.
  • Win condition: Connect your two sides with an unbroken chain of your stones. First to do so wins.

The Swap Rule (Tournament Standard)

In competitive Hex, Black has a significant advantage (the first player can always win with perfect play — Nash proved this). The swap rule balances it: after Black's first move, White may choose to swap colors (taking Black's position and stone) or continue as White. This makes the game fair.

Strategy

  • Connect your edges. Every stone should work toward linking your two sides. Avoid playing solely for local battles.
  • Threats must match. A threat on the left must be answered on the left, a threat on the right on the right. This "ladder" principle drives the game.
  • Virtual connections. Two stones that are one cell apart (with an empty cell between them) form a "virtual connection" — the opponent cannot block both sides simultaneously.
  • Double threats. A stone that creates two separate winning threats is often unbeatable. This is Hex's equivalent of a fork in chess.
  • Central stones are powerful. Control of the board center gives you access to both sides. Strong players prioritize the center diamond.
  • Defensive play often means extending your opponent's shortest path, forcing them to waste moves.

Why It Matters

Hex is one of the few games with a known mathematical solution (first player wins with perfect play), yet the actual winning strategy is unknown. No one has ever found a constructive proof — we know the first player must win, but not how. Nash's original proof used a topological argument called the "Steinitz exchange principle" from game theory.

The game is a rare intersection of:

  • Combinatorial game theory — no draws, no captures, perfect information
  • Topology — the "hex theorem" states that exactly one player must win, proven via the Jordan curve theorem
  • Artificial intelligence — MoHex, the strongest Hex AI, uses Monte Carlo tree search. Before MCTS, computers were weaker than strong humans at Hex.

Famous Games

  • Nash vs. the Princeton common room — John Nash reportedly never lost at Princeton, defeating all challengers. No recorded games survive.
  • Piet Hein's original demonstrations — Hein demonstrated Polygon at the 1942 Niels Bohr Institute Christmas party. No records survive.
  • MoHex vs. top human players — In 2015, MoHex 2.0 (our engine) defeated top human players, achieving super-human strength. Before that, Hex was one of the last board games where humans beat computers.
  • Little Golem tournaments — The online server Little Golem hosted Hex tournaments from 2001-2020. Game records from these tournaments form the basis of most modern Hex AI training.
