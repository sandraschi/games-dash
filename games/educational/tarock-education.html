📜 History of Tarock

Renaissance Origins

Tarock (Tarot) was invented in northern Italy in the early 15th century — first documented in Ferrara around 1440, making it one of the oldest card games still played. The deck was created for trick-taking games ("tarocchi"), and the tarot cards were used for gaming long before they were ever used for fortune-telling.

To Vienna with the Habsburgs

Tarock spread through the Habsburg Empire in the 17th and 18th centuries. It became especially beloved in Vienna, where it was played in coffee houses, aristocratic salons, and the imperial court itself. Tarock evenings ("Tarockabende") became a cherished Viennese social institution, and the game is woven into Austrian literature and culture.

The Austrian Variants

Over time, Austria developed its own distinctive variants. The two dominant ones are Königrufen (widespread across Austria, 54 cards) and Zwanzigerrufen (dominant in eastern Austria, 40 cards, faster and simpler). This game implements 2-player Zwanzigerrufen — the eastern Austrian classic.

The Deck

Zwanzigerrufen uses 40 cards taken from the 54-card Tarock deck:

  • 20 Tarocks: numbered I to XXI in Roman numerals plus the unnumbered Sküs (the "Gstieß", highest card). Tarocks II and III are removed for Zwanzigerrufen.
  • 20 Suit cards: 4 suits (♠ ♥ ♦ ♣) with 5 cards each: König (K), Dame (Q), Reiter (R), Bube (B) and a "Glatze" (blank, 0 points). The Glatze is the 10 for ♠/♣ and the Ace for ♥/♦.
Sküs XXI X I K♠ Q♥ R♦ B♣ 10♠ A♥

The Trull are the three special honors: Sküs, XXI (the Mond) and I (the Pagat) — each worth 5 points and subject to special rules.

📋 Rules of Zwanzigerrufen

Card Values

  • Tarocks: 1 point each; the Trull (Sküs, XXI Mond, I Pagat) are worth 5 points each
  • König: 5 points
  • Dame: 4 points
  • Reiter (Cavall): 3 points
  • Bube: 2 points
  • Glatze: 0 points

The deck totals 88 points. The winner of a hand needs 45 points (more than half).

Deal & Play

  • Each player gets 10 cards (in 4-player play) — there is no talon in Zwanzigerrufen. In this 2-player version all 40 cards are dealt (20 each) so every point is in play.
  • The Vorhand (forehand) leads the first trick; the trick winner leads the next
  • Tarocks are permanent trumps and beat every suit card; a higher tarock beats a lower one
  • Farbzwang: you must follow the led suit if you can
  • Trumpfzwang: if you cannot follow suit, you must play a tarock if you have one
  • No Stichzwang: you are never forced to beat the current best card
  • Suit cards rank K > Q > R > B > Glatze (the Glatze is the weakest card of its suit)

Bidding

  • Rufer (normal game, 1 point): the Vorhand calls the XX (the 20th trump); its holder becomes their partner. In 2-player play, the declarer plays alone (the partner call is a 4-player mechanic).
  • Farbensolo (4 points): the declarer plays alone; tarocks do not trump suits, and may only be played once your suit cards are gone.
  • Solo (4 points): the declarer plays alone with full trump power; all premiums count double.
  • Solo overbids Farbensolo overbids Rufer. If everyone passes, the hand is redealt.

Premiums

  • Trull (1 point): holding Sküs, Mond and Pagat in your opening hand
  • 4 Könige (2 points): holding all four Kings in your opening hand
  • Mond gefangen (1 point): catching the Mond by beating it with the Sküs
  • Pagat Ultimo (1 silent, 2 declared): winning the last trick with the Pagat
  • Absolut (1 silent, 2 declared): holding the opponent to 32 points or less
  • Valat (6 points): winning all 20 tricks (2-player)

The declarer may announce Absolut or Pagat Ultimo before play for double value — a declared premium that fails is scored for the opponent.

2-Player Adaptation (this game)

Zwanzigerrufen is normally a 4-player game with partner calling. In this 2-player version all 40 cards are dealt (20 each), the declarer always plays alone, and the XX-call is shown for flavor (it determines which trump you "call"). Dealer and Vorhand rotate each hand; the first player to ±10 match points wins the match.

🎯 Strategy & Tactics

When to Bid

  • Rufer with about 5+ tarocks and 30+ points in hand
  • Solo with 8+ tarocks, especially with 2+ Trull cards
  • Farbensolo with a suit-heavy hand (6+ suit cards) and few points — refuse the trump game and fight with suits
  • Don't overbid weak hands: a lost Solo costs 4 points, not 1

Card Play

  • Save your trumps: don't waste high tarocks on small tricks. Use them to win tricks containing Kings, Tens-worth cards, or the opponent's high points.
  • Dump blanks early: play Glatzen and low tarocks first — they're worth nothing and only clog your hand.
  • Count the tarocks: track which tarocks have fallen. Knowing the Sküs and top trumps are gone tells you when a mid tarock wins.
  • Protect the Mond: if the Sküs is still out, playing the Mond is dangerous — it can be caught for a premium. Hold it until the Sküs has fallen.
  • Mondfang hunting: hold the Sküs to catch the Mond. The Sküs also beats everything else, so it doubles as the ultimate trump.
  • Pagat Ultimo: if you hold the Pagat, try to win the last trick with it. Keep it as long as possible — if you're forced to play it early, the premium is gone.
Follow-suit discipline: the obligations (Farbzwang/Trumpfzwang) mean you often can't choose your card. Plan your leads so that your forced plays fall in your favor — e.g. lead a suit you hold long and weak, forcing the opponent to spend strength following.

🌐 Tarock Variants

Zwanzigerrufen (40 cards)

Dominant in eastern Austria (Vienna and surroundings). Faster and simpler than Königrufen: no talon, simplified scoring, one "Besserrufer" (Pagat Ultimo). This is the variant implemented in this game.

Königrufen (54 cards)

The most widespread Austrian Tarock variant. Uses the full 54-card deck (22 tarocks, 4 suits × 8 cards) and has more bidding options: Dreier, Unterer, Bettler, Piccolo and their "Ouvert" (open) versions, plus a talon that the declarer may take. Called "Königrufen" because the declarer calls a King as their partner.

Dreierschnapsen / other dialects

Regional rule sets across Austria ("Spieldialekte") differ in details — which trumps are callable, premium values, whether stacking and "Zusammenwerfen" (throwing in with no tarocks) are allowed. Clubs like the HKSW publish their own authoritative rule books.

International Tarot

Outside Austria, the 78-card French Tarot (played with bidding and points) remains popular in France, and Italian Tarocchi variants survive in Piedmont and Sicily. All of them descend from the same Renaissance ancestor — but the Austrian game is the one that made Tarock a national treasure.
